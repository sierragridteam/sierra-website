On some calm mornings a handheld radio or a car's FM radio picks up a station from much farther away than it usually reaches. By midmorning the station has faded. The cause is the air between the two antennas, which on those mornings bends the signal farther than usual.

## How air bends a signal

The [September post on antenna height](/news/2026-09-10-canyon-radio-height) set out the usual rule for VHF and UHF: the signals travel close to a straight line, and a radio's reach ends near its horizon. That rule already carries a small correction for the air. Meteorologists and radio engineers describe the effect with a number called refractivity, which normally falls with height. A wave front moves slightly slower through the denser air near the ground, so it bends gently back toward the Earth. In typical conditions, according to [course material from the Naval Postgraduate School](https://www.oc.nps.edu/NWDC_EM_Course/course_materials/module3_1.html), radio waves bend more than light, so the radio horizon sits a little beyond the visible one. Engineers account for this by treating the Earth's radius as four-thirds of its true size, and the horizon rule of thumb is built on that.

## When the bend keeps pace with the Earth

The bending grows when refractivity falls off with height faster than usual. The common cause is a temperature inversion: a layer in which the air gets warmer with height. The same course notes that inversions are most likely in high-pressure systems away from fronts and storms. Once refractivity drops by more than 157 units for each kilometer of height, the layer becomes a trapping layer. A signal inside it bends back toward the ground faster than the Earth curves away below it, and it stays in the layer. That trapped path is a duct. A broadcast engineer, [writing in TV Technology](https://www.tvtechnology.com/opinions/enhanced-propagation-of-tv-signals), described a signal in a duct as propagating "more like it is in a leaky waveguide than traveling through free space."

Higher frequencies are trapped more easily. The same article notes that some ducts carry UHF or microwave signals but not VHF, and [Electronics Notes](https://www.electronics-notes.com/articles/antennas-propagation/tropospheric/tropo-lifts-ducting-enhanced-conditions.php) says the enhancement is weaker in the lower part of the VHF range. When an inversion forms as the ground cools overnight, the extra range can last into the early morning and fade later as the air warms.

## The Pacific path

Some of the longest ducts on record run over the ocean between Hawaii and California. Paul Lieb, KH6HME, kept beacons at 8,200 feet on the slopes of Mauna Loa and set [many ducting distance records](https://www.arrl.org/news/vhf-pioneer-paul-lieb-kh6hme-sk), including a 2-meter contact on 144.170 MHz across 2,954 miles in 1999. In June 2015, an operator in a rental car at 5,200 feet on the Big Island and another at a station near Orange, California, [made contacts on 2.3 and 3.4 GHz](https://www.arrl.org/news/new-world-distance-records-set-on-2-3-and-3-4-ghz-ham-bands) over more than 2,495 miles.

## The fog in the valley

Foothill residents can see an inversion in winter under the Central Valley's tule fog. Cool, moist air sits beneath warmer air, and in one December 2025 episode [a CBS Sacramento meteorologist compared it to a lid on a pot](https://www.cbsnews.com/sacramento/news/sacramento-tule-fog-foothills-sunshine), with the top of the fog around 1,200 feet. The TV Technology article says inversions that form close to the ground at night are too low to make effective ducts, and that ducting comes from inversions between about 1,500 and 4,900 feet (450 to 1,500 meters) above the ground.

## Reading a range test

Ducting can be forecast. William Hepburn's [tropospheric ducting index](https://dxinfocentre.com/propagation/hti.htm) rates the bending expected over an area, and its notes say local terrain has a great impact, because the effect happens in the lowest part of the atmosphere; signal strength can differ between places a few miles apart.

For anyone testing their own radios, the timing of the test matters. A path that works on a still, high-pressure morning may be using extra range that the weather supplied that day, so repeat the test on an ordinary afternoon before relying on the result.
